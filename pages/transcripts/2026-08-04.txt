Layer Lines Weekly — Materialise: The Company That Bet on Software Before Anyone Knew Software Was the Bet
Episode 16 · 2026-08-04

Alex: Today's company is one that most people in 3D printing have touched without ever buying a single thing from them directly. Here's my thesis: Materialise is the company that figured out, way back in 1990, that the durable money in additive manufacturing was going to be in software and services, not in the printers themselves.

Jordan: And they figured it out so early that for a long time nobody was even competing with them for it. This is a Belgian company, founded by a guy named Fried Vancraen, that ended up sitting in the workflow of a huge chunk of industrial 3D printing while also quietly becoming a serious medical device company.

Alex: Which is such a weird combination on paper. Build preparation software and custom skull implants, under one roof.

Jordan: It makes more sense than it sounds, and we'll get to why. We've got the origin story, the products that actually explain the company, a CEO who's been making some pretty sharp cuts, and a big open question: can a company this focused on margins and discipline actually grow again?

Alex: One caveat before we start. Some of the big headline numbers you see attached to this company, revenue figures, employee counts, patent counts, we could not independently pin down in our research. So when we cite scale, treat it as the company's own framing unless we say otherwise.

Alex: So, 1990. For context, that's before the web browser exists. Stereolithography is a few years old. And Fried Vancraen buys a 3D printer and starts a company around it in Leuven, Belgium. Now, I'll flag that the exact founding-year detail comes from the company's own telling, their about page just says over thirty years of experience, but the timeline holds together.

Jordan: And what matters isn't the year, it's what he did with the machine. Because in 1990, a 3D printer is an expensive, temperamental prototyping tool. The obvious business is: print parts for people. Which they did. But the less obvious insight was that the machines were only half the problem.

Alex: What do you mean, half the problem?

Jordan: Getting a CAD file into a state where a printer can actually build it. Fixing broken geometry, orienting parts, generating supports, nesting builds. In the early days that was miserable, manual work. Materialise built software to solve their own service bureau's problems, and then realized everyone else running these machines had the same problems.

Alex: So the service business becomes a kind of laboratory for the software business. They're their own first customer.

Jordan: That's the founding bet in one sentence. Sell the parts, but also sell the tools you built to make the parts. And run both at once for three decades.

Alex: It clearly worked out for the founders personally. Forbes ran a piece in 2021 with the headline about how Materialise's founding couple 3D printed a billion-dollar fortune. Which is a great headline, but worth noting that's a stock-based valuation from a moment when 3D printing stocks were flying. That number is paper wealth at a peak, not cash in a vault.

Jordan: And 2021 was a strange year for AM valuations across the board. But the underlying point stands: this went from one printer in Belgium to a publicly traded company on Nasdaq. That's a real journey regardless of where the stock sits on any given day.

Jordan: The other thing about starting in 1990 is you survive every hype cycle this industry has produced. The consumer 3D printing bubble, the industrial hype wave, the SPAC era. Materialise predates all of it and outlived most of the companies born in each one.

Alex: There's a survivorship credential there that's hard to fake. Okay, so let's get into what they actually make, because I think the products are where this company stops being abstract.

Alex: The product everyone starts with is Magics. This is their build preparation software, and the company calls it an industry standard. I want to be careful with that phrase because it's their marketing language, and we don't have an independent survey backing it up.

Jordan: Fair to flag. What I'll say from having spent time around industrial shops and now teaching this stuff: Magics shows up a lot. Anecdotally, it's the tool people reach for when they're preparing serious metal or polymer builds. But that's my anecdote, not data, and anecdotes are exactly how marketing claims launder themselves into facts.

Alex: What does it actually do that a free slicer doesn't? Because listeners with a desktop machine at home might be thinking, I already have build prep software.

Jordan: The gap is enormous. A hobby slicer takes one clean STL and makes G-code for one machine. Industrial build prep means repairing geometry that came out of five different CAD systems, nesting dozens of parts in a single powder bed so you don't waste a two-thousand-euro build, generating supports that a technician can actually remove, and doing it repeatably with a paper trail. When a build failure costs you a day and a pile of metal powder, that software pays for itself fast.

Alex: The newer layer on top of that is CO-AM, which the company describes as a production management platform. Basically software for running a whole additive factory rather than a single build.

Jordan: Order intake, scheduling, traceability, quality management across a fleet of machines. There's a case study on their site about EOS using CO-AM to manage production at scale, which, take with the usual case-study salt, but it tells you who the target customer is. And this got a real update recently: trade coverage at 3DPrint.com confirmed they launched new CO-AM tools in November 2025 aimed specifically at serial manufacturing.

Alex: Serial manufacturing meaning repeat production of the same part, not one-off prototypes.

Jordan: Which is where the whole industry keeps saying it wants to go, and where the software requirements get much stricter. Prototyping tolerates chaos. Production doesn't.

Alex: Then there's the medical side, which honestly deserves its own episode. The recent headline there: in February 2026, per coverage at All3DP, Materialise launched custom PEEK implants for cranio-maxillofacial surgery. So patient-specific implants for skulls and faces, printed in a high-performance polymer.

Jordan: And notice what connects all three of these products. Magics, CO-AM, custom implants. They're all about controlling the workflow between a digital design and a physical part someone is willing to trust. An implant is just the most extreme version of that: the qualification burden is brutal, and the part has to fit one specific human. That's the same core competency as build prep, pushed to its logical limit.

Alex: That's actually the answer to my question from the top. Software and skull implants aren't two businesses. They're one competency, workflow control, sold at two very different price points.

Alex: Let's talk about who's running this. Fried Vancraen founded it and led it for most of its history, but the current CEO is Brigitte de Vet. And she's been giving interviews, there's one at All3DP and coverage at VoxelMatters, where she frames 3D printing as a complementary technology. Not a revolution that replaces everything, but one tool among many.

Jordan: Which sounds like a small rhetorical choice, but in this industry it's almost heretical. The default CEO script in additive for fifteen years was disruption-of-all-manufacturing. Saying complementary out loud is a signal that you're pricing your own strategy against reality rather than against a pitch deck.

Alex: And the actions match the rhetoric. Multiple outlets, TCT Magazine, GlobeNewswire, Quiver Quantitative, confirmed that under her leadership Materialise transferred two businesses out to their own management teams: RapidFit and the eyewear business.

Jordan: Two readings of that. The generous one is portfolio discipline: those units grow better independent, and the core company focuses on software and medical. The less generous one is that you're shedding businesses that weren't performing and dressing it up as strategy. The public record supports the transfers themselves; the motive is interpretation either way.

Alex: The framing around it in coverage is margin improvement despite flat revenue, though I'll note the specific margin numbers weren't in our verified material, so hold that loosely.

Jordan: Cutting your way to better margins is a real skill and a legitimate strategy. It's also the easy half. The hard half is what you do after the pruning, and that's the part we can't judge yet.

Jordan: One honest limitation here: beyond de Vet's public interviews and the founder story, we don't have good sourcing on the broader executive bench, ownership structure, or internal culture. So we're not going to invent color there. What we can say is that the visible leadership moves, the spin-offs, the complementary framing, all point in one consistent direction: narrow the company down to what it's best at.

Alex: Which is a nice segue, because what exactly is it best at? What's the actual moat here?

Alex: The company's pitch, and you can read this on their partnerships pages, is that they're machine-agnostic. Their software connects to printers from lots of different manufacturers through what they call Build Processors, basically translation layers between their software and each machine's native language. I've seen the claim that this covers hundreds of printers, but I want to flag we could not verify that number, so let's just say many.

Jordan: The number matters less than the position. Think about what a Build Processor actually requires: the machine OEM has to hand you enough access to their hardware that your software can drive it well. HP did that, there's a long-running partnership there going back to at least a 2017 interview on Materialise's own site, plus independent coverage at Engineering.com in 2023. EOS did it too, per that CO-AM case study. Each one of those relationships took years to build, and that accumulated trust is genuinely hard to replicate.

Alex: It's the Switzerland strategy. Everyone else is fighting to sell machines, and Materialise sells the neutral layer that all the machines have to talk through.

Jordan: With one big vulnerability, and it's worth sitting on. Neutrality only works if the OEMs keep cooperating. Every major printer manufacturer would love to own its own software stack end to end, because software margins beat hardware margins. If the big OEMs decide their bundled software is good enough, the neutral layer gets squeezed from both sides.

Alex: It's a funny mirror image of the company we profiled a few weeks back, Stratasys, which built its whole model on keeping people inside its ecosystem. Materialise built its model on being the thing that works across everyone's ecosystem. Two opposite bets on the same question of openness.

Jordan: And notably, the open bet has been cheaper to maintain. You don't have to fund hardware R&D or carry factory overhead to be the workflow layer.

Jordan: The other pillar of the moat is quality infrastructure. They market a quality management system alongside the software, and on the medical side they've spent decades building regulatory muscle. That's less visible than a clever algorithm, but if you've ever tried to qualify an additive part for anything safety-critical, you know the paperwork is often harder than the printing. A company that has already walked implants through regulators has an asset most software startups can't buy.

Alex: What about the patent side? The number that floats around is 488 patents, but again, we couldn't source that independently.

Jordan: So treat it as a company claim. And honestly, patent counts are a weak proxy for moat anyway. I'd weight the OEM relationships and the medical regulatory track record far above the patent portfolio. Those are the things a competitor can't replicate in a funding cycle.

Alex: So the moat, stripped of the marketing language: trusted neutral position between OEMs, workflow software that shops already depend on, and regulatory experience in medical. Real, but not invulnerable.
