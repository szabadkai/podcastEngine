Alex: So TDK is acquiring a San Diego company called Fabric8Labs for up to four hundred million dollars. This was all over the trades this week — TCT, VoxelMatters, 3DPrint all had it. And the technology is the part I keep coming back to, because it's not laser-melting metal. It's something called ECAM. Jordan, this is your wheelhouse — what is it actually doing?

Jordan: Okay, so quick version for anyone who hasn't gone down this particular rabbit hole. The metal printing most people picture is powder bed fusion — you spread a layer of metal powder, blast it with a laser, melt it, repeat. Lots of heat, lots of energy, lots of stress in the part. ECAM stands for electrochemical additive manufacturing, and it's a completely different animal. It's basically electroplating, but with control. You're depositing metal out of a solution, atom by atom, at room temperature.

Alex: Room temperature. So no melting at all.

Jordan: None. And that matters more than it sounds. When you melt and resolidify metal, you get residual stress, you get grain structure problems, you get warping. If you can grow the part electrochemically instead, you sidestep a whole category of headaches. And critically — you can make really fine, really pure copper structures. Which is the part that explains why TDK wanted it.

Alex: Right, because this is being framed as an AI infrastructure play, which I'll admit took me a second. What does a metal printer have to do with AI?

Jordan: Heat. AI data centers run these chips that throw off absurd amounts of heat, and the bottleneck on building more of them is cooling. High-purity copper is the gold standard for thermal management — it pulls heat away beautifully. And if ECAM can print intricate copper cooling structures that you couldn't machine conventionally, then suddenly you've got a manufacturing process aimed straight at the hottest market on the planet, literally and financially.

Alex: Okay, so that's the why-should-I-care for me — it's not 'cool new printer,' it's 'a giant just bet that this is how you cool the AI boom.' That reframes it completely.

Jordan: And it puts it in scale. Four hundred million — for context, Desktop Metal paid around five seventy-five for ExOne, and that was a landmark deal. Stratasys-Origin was a hundred. So this is genuinely one of the biggest additive acquisitions in years. That's not a tuck-in. That's a strategic commitment.

Alex: I do want to flag one thing — every write-up calls ECAM 'production-ready.' Is that an established fact, or is that TDK's implied bet?

Jordan: That's the bet, and I'm glad you caught it. None of the sources cite independent production volumes or customer case studies. So when you read 'production-ready,' the honest translation is: a very sophisticated buyer did its due diligence and concluded it's close enough to write a nine-figure check. That's a strong signal — TDK doesn't fling money around — but it's their assessment, not a benchmark anyone published.

Alex: Which is a fair distinction, but I don't want to over-weight it either. The check is the validation. A company like that doesn't acquire a process and then discover it doesn't work.

Jordan: No, exactly. And here's the part I find genuinely encouraging — for years the metal AM story was 'someday this will matter for production.' This is a someday-arrived story. A non-additive electronics giant looked at a printing process and saw it as core to their roadmap, not a side experiment. That's the maturation we keep talking about, happening in real money.

Alex: And it folds Fabric8Labs into TDK's global manufacturing network, which is the thing a startup almost never gets — instant scale, instant supply chain. That's the bit that makes me think this actually ships product rather than dying in a lab.

Jordan: That's the strongest argument for it being real. A clever process in a startup is a science project. A clever process inside a company that already makes billions of electronic components is a product line. The technology was always interesting; the acquisition is what could make it matter.

Alex: Speaking of stuff getting more accessible — Formlabs launched the Fuse X1 this week. Large-format SLS, starts at eighty-four thousand nine hundred ninety-nine. This was on 3D Printing Industry, TCT, Fabbaloo. And the headline number is that it's roughly half the cost of comparable industrial systems.

Jordan: Quick translation — SLS is selective laser sintering. You've got a bed of nylon powder, a laser fuses it layer by layer, and the unfused powder around your part acts as its own support. No support structures to snap off. It's fantastic for functional plastic parts. And industrial SLS has historically started at a hundred fifty, two hundred K and up, so eighty-five is genuinely aggressive.

Alex: They're also claiming 3x throughput and saying it's already running at Tesla's Gigafactory.

Jordan: And this is where I put my hand up. Three-x compared to what? Their own baseline is the Fuse 1, which is a compact benchtop machine. So 3x faster than a small benchtop unit is not the same as 3x faster than an EOS production system. It's Formlabs' own number, no independent test methodology, no stated conditions. It might be great — but 'three times' is a comparison floating in space until they tell you what the baseline is.

Alex: And the 'half the cost' thing — that's machine price, right? Not total cost of ownership.

Jordan: Right, and that's the one that bites people. With SLS your real cost is powder, post-processing, the powder handling setup, the service contract. The sticker is half — is the running cost half? Nobody's said. I've watched plenty of teams buy a 'cheap' industrial machine and then discover the consumables budget is where the money actually lives. So: promising machine, real price drop, just don't let the marketing math do your purchasing decision for you.

Alex: The Tesla mention is also single-source via Formlabs — no scale, no detail on what they're printing.

Jordan: It's a logo on a slide until we know more. 'Used at the Gigafactory' could mean a production cell or it could mean one machine in a corner printing jigs. Both are fine! But they're very different stories.

Alex: Let's do the one that actually made me go 'ooh' — Oak Ridge developed a printable aluminum alloy called DuAlumin-3D that holds its strength at four hundred degrees C. That was on 3D Printing Industry. Four hundred degrees is wild for aluminum, isn't it?

Jordan: It really is. Most aluminum alloys start losing the plot above two hundred, two-fifty C. They go soft. So the whole reason aluminum gets ruled out of hot applications — engine internals, certain aerospace parts — is exactly this. If you've got an aluminum that stays strong at four hundred, you've just handed engineers a lightweight option in places they could only use steel or titanium before.

Alex: They took it from concept to full-scale automotive piston prototypes in under three years, which for a national lab feels fast.

Jordan: It is fast, and ORNL is about as credible a source as exists. The thing I want before I get too excited — the article gives the claim but not the numbers. Retains strength how? Fifty percent of room-temperature strength? Eighty? For how long under load? Because four hundred C, sustained, for a part that's load-bearing is a very different ask than a quick thermal spike. The mechanism matters and it's not spelled out.

Alex: So filed under 'genuinely exciting, still research-stage.'

Jordan: Exactly that. Lab success to a qualified, costed, production powder is a long road — I've watched alloys that looked incredible in a paper take a decade to show up in anyone's machine. But this is the good kind of news. Real lab, real prototype, clear use case. I'd just hold the champagne until the data sheet lands.

Jordan: Alright, my corner of the show. Two aerospace stories this week and they're a great pairing, because one is what mature additive looks like and the other is what early additive looks like dressed up as mature. Alex: Microvanes — these are little aerodynamic fins?

Jordan: Small aerodynamic devices, yeah. You bolt them on near the rear of the aircraft and they smooth out the airflow, reduce drag, and you save fuel. The aerodynamics isn't new — that's established. What's new is the manufacturing approach. Instead of tooling up a traditional production line for a part you're putting on a few hundred planes, you print them. No retooling, no minimum order quantity headaches.

Alex: And 222 aircraft — that's a lot of confidence in printed parts on something that flies.

Jordan: It is, and that's why I like it. This is fleet-wide deployment on an operational military aircraft. That's not 'we printed a demo part once.' Now — fair caveat — these are small, non-structural add-ons. If a microvane fails it's not the same as a turbine blade letting go. So it's a smart entry point. Low risk, real benefit, exactly where you'd want additive to prove itself on a fleet.

Alex: The one-year timeline — believable?

Alex: Now the second one — Lockheed Skunk Works and Divergent built a drone.

And the headline reads like a triumph, and it might be one, but I want to be careful about what 'first article' means, because it's doing a lot of work in that sentence.

Alex: Translate 'first article' for me.

Jordan: First article is the first physical unit you build off your design. One. It is not a flight-qualified, certified, production-ready aircraft. So 'concept to first article in under a year' could mean they designed and fabricated one airframe in twelve months — genuinely fast — or it could be folding in requirements, design reviews, and testing. The scope is unclear, and that gap is the difference between 'impressive prototype' and 'we can manufacture these.'

Alex: And Divergent — their whole thing is automotive structures, right? The car chassis stuff.

Jordan: That's their proven domain — their DAPS platform makes lightweight vehicle structures. Building a hundred that fly missions is manufacturing. Don't confuse the two.

Alex: There's a nice thread between these and the qualification story too, isn't there?

Jordan: There is — that's the third one. America Makes and NCDMM awarded two million dollars to a project led by Dyndrite to build an AI-based framework for material qualification in laser powder bed fusion. 3D Printing Industry and TCT both covered it. Alex: Qualification being — proving the material does what you say, consistently enough to certify it?

Jordan: Exactly. In aerospace, medical, defense — you can't just print a part and bolt it on. You have to prove the material's properties are reliable, batch after batch, machine after machine. That qualification process is slow and brutally expensive, and it's the single biggest thing holding additive back in regulated industries. So the idea of using AI to identify and quantify the risk faster — that's aimed at the real bottleneck. It's just early. Two million, project's starting, no results. Jordan: Two of them. There's no completed hundred-meter structure. There's no completed structure at any height cited.

Alex: So has it actually printed anything?

Jordan: That is exactly the question, and the source doesn't answer it. And here's why it matters — a crane sways. It deflects in wind. At a hundred meters up you've got wind loads, you've got vibration, you've got the problem of pumping concrete that high and getting it to cure properly before the next layer. Gantry systems are rigid precisely to avoid this. Crane-mounting trades your rigidity away for height, and none of those engineering problems are addressed in the announcement. That's not a footnote — that's the entire hard part of the idea.
