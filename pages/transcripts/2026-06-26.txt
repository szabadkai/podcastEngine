Jordan: Oh I have feelings about that last one. Feelings involving explosive powder. We'll get there.

Alex: So the big story. Oak Ridge National Lab — 3DPrintingIndustry and 3DPrint both had it this week — came out with this origami-inspired composite process. And the framing I keep seeing is "ninety percent cheaper." But the actually clever bit isn't the cost number. Jordan, walk me through what they're actually doing, because when I read it I went "wait, that's a sneaky-smart move."

Jordan: So normally, to make a big composite structural part — think an aircraft panel, a boat hull section — you build a mold. A tool. And the tool is brutally expensive. It's machined, it's huge, it takes weeks, and if you change the design you eat that cost again. The mold is the villain in composites manufacturing, full stop.

Alex: And these molds aren't a one-off cost, right? They wear out, you store them, you've got a warehouse full of tooling for parts you might never make again.

Jordan: Exactly. So ORNL's trick is: print your composite material flat, directly onto a flexible fabric substrate. Lay it all down in two dimensions, where printing is fast and easy and cheap. Then you fold it — origami-style — into the final 3D shape. No mold. The fold lines do the work the tool used to do.

Alex: Okay that's genuinely beautiful as an idea. It's the move where the hard problem — making a complex curved shape — gets converted into an easy problem you already know how to solve. Print flat, then fold. Why does that save ninety percent though? That's the number that's doing a lot of lifting.

Jordan: And here's where I put the brakes on a little, because this is the soft spot. Ninety percent against what? ORNL is a credible lab — they usually do give you a comparative baseline — but a ninety percent figure is almost always one specific case study. One part, one geometry, compared to one conventional process. It is not "composites are now ninety percent cheaper across the board."

Alex: So it's a real number, just a narrow one. The headline does that thing where it takes the best slide from the deck and makes it the law of the land.

Jordan: Right. And the question I always ask my students with stuff like this — one of them actually asked me almost word for word this week — is "if it's ninety percent cheaper, why isn't everyone already doing it?" And the answer is usually readiness level. This is research. It's a demonstration. Folding a flat panel into a structural part means your fold zones have to carry load, survive fatigue, not delaminate.

Alex: Ah — because a fold is a stress concentration. You're deliberately creating the exact feature that engineers normally spend their lives trying to avoid.

Jordan: You've got it. The crease is where it wants to fail. So the magic is in making the crease as strong as the rest, and that's the hard, unglamorous work that turns a cool demo into a qualified part. That's years, not months. The "large-scale production" claim in the writeup is aspiration, not a shipping capability.

Alex: But I don't want to be too sour on it, because the core idea is the kind of thing that does eventually change a whole industry. Eliminating tooling isn't a tweak. It's the thing that's been gatekeeping composites away from low-volume and custom work forever.

Jordan: No, I'm with you. This is the good kind of research — it attacks the real cost driver instead of shaving milliseconds off a print. If they nail the fold mechanics, the people who win aren't necessarily aerospace primes. It's the smaller shops who could never justify a half-million-dollar mold for a fifty-part run. That's where I'd watch this land first.

Alex: So the honest version of the headline is: brilliant idea, real cost upside on the right part, still in the lab, and the number you saw is one case study wearing a cape.

Jordan: Perfectly put. File it under "call me when there's a folded part that's flown."

Alex: Speaking of getting industrial gear to smaller shops — let's do hardware. ExOne launched the S-Print Pro, which 3DPrint and TCT both covered. It's a compact sand binder jetting machine, under twelve square meters of floor space, aimed squarely at small foundries and pattern shops. And for anyone who hasn't gone down this rabbit hole, Jordan, what is sand printing even doing?

Jordan: So this is one of the oldest, most boring-but-brilliant uses of AM, and I love it. You're not printing the final metal part. You're printing the sand mold and the cores that you then pour molten metal into. Binder jetting lays down sand, squirts a binder to glue it in the right shape, layer by layer, and out comes a mold that used to take a skilled patternmaker ages to build by hand.

Alex: So it's not replacing casting, it's replacing the hand-tooling that feeds casting. That's a much easier sell to a foundry — you don't have to convince them to abandon a process they've trusted for a century, you just hand them better molds, faster.

Jordan: Exactly, and that's why I think this category is quietly one of the most successful corners of AM. The catch on the S-Print Pro is the word "affordable." The footprint spec is verified — under twelve square meters, real number. But "affordable" and "accessible"? No price in the announcement. And in foundry economics, sticker price barely matters.

Alex: Because it's all total cost of ownership. The binder, the sand, the post-processing, the payback period.

Jordan: Right. A small foundry doesn't ask "what's the printer cost," they ask "how many molds a month until this thing pays for itself, and can I keep it fed." Until ExOne puts a number and a payback story next to "affordable," it's a marketing word. Good machine concept, incomplete pitch.

If it's a relabeled blend at a premium because the name's on it? Pass. I want the datasheet, not the brand.

Alex: And then the third one, which has the spiciest headline — Swiss researchers, metal AM repair, "quadruples the service life of aging steel bridges." Four times. That's a big claim about a thing that, if it falls down, is a catastrophe.

Jordan: So the underlying tech is solid and verified — using directed energy deposition to repair and reinforce steel. DED, for the uninitiated, is basically a robot arm feeding metal wire or powder into a melt pool, building material up where it's worn or cracked, like very high-tech welding. Alex: Let's come back down to the bench. Two maker stories I'm into. First — there's a workflow going around for turning Gaussian Splats into printable models. So you photograph a building, a landmark, your house, and turn it into a little physical replica. As someone who prints terrain for tabletop, my brain immediately went "I could print my actual neighborhood."

Jordan: Okay quick translation, because "Gaussian Splat" sounds like a Magic card. It's a newer way of reconstructing a 3D scene from photos — instead of a solid mesh, it represents the scene as a cloud of little fuzzy colored blobs. Looks gorgeous on screen, captures lighting and detail beautifully. The catch is it's not a watertight solid.

Alex: Ah — and a printer needs a watertight solid. It needs to know what's inside and what's outside. A cloud of blobs doesn't tell you that.

Jordan: Right. So the splat-to-mesh tools exist, but the meshes they spit out are often hollow, full of holes, weird floating bits, surfaces with no thickness. Which means the "convert and print" pipeline has a big invisible step in the middle labeled "now spend an hour in your mesh repair software."

Alex: Which honestly tracks with every photogrammetry thing I've ever tried. The capture looks magic, then you open it up and there's a hole where the roof should be and the whole back of the building is a smear because you only walked around the front.

Jordan: And that's the real maker lesson hiding in this: the quality is set the moment you take the photos. Good even coverage, lots of angles, consistent light — get that right and the cleanup is minutes. Get lazy and you're sculpting the missing half by hand. The tool's only as good as the walk-around you did.

Alex: Second one, and this is more your strength-of-materials world — Hackaday ran a piece this week investigating annealing as a fix for poor carbon-fiber adhesion in FDM prints. Which surprised me, because I always assumed carbon fiber filament made things stronger, full stop.

Jordan: Common misconception, and it's a good one to bust. The chopped carbon fiber stiffens the filament — better rigidity, less warping. But those little fibers actually interrupt the bonding between layers. So you often get a part that's stiffer in-plane but weaker in the Z direction, between layers. The fibers help one thing and hurt another.

Alex: And annealing is — you bake the finished part to let the polymer reorganize and bond better? I've heard people talk about it but never tried it because I'm scared of what happens.

Jordan: You've got the right instinct to be scared. Annealing — heating the part to relax and re-crystallize the polymer — can genuinely improve layer adhesion and strength. Hackaday actually tested it. But the thing that bites everyone: as it re-crystallizes, it shrinks and can warp. So you can come out with a stronger part that no longer fits where it was supposed to go.

Alex: Which is the most heartbreaking failure mode — it worked, and that's the problem. The part's stronger and three percent smaller and now your bracket doesn't bolt up.

Jordan: Alright. The one we've been waiting for. The GLB DP-C1 — a company claiming a safe, affordable, laser-based metal 3D printer for your home workshop. All3DP, to their credit, ran it with a giant question mark. And my professional reaction, as someone who's stood next to industrial metal machines, is: absolutely not.

Alex: Okay but let me play the optimist for a second, because that's my job and someone has to. We laughed at consumer resin once. We laughed at sub-thousand-dollar CoreXY. Why is "consumer metal" automatically a fantasy and not just the next thing that gets democratized?

Jordan: Because the barrier here isn't cost or cleverness — it's physics and chemistry. Laser powder bed fusion uses fine metal powder. Fine metal powder is an inhalation hazard and, in the right particle size, genuinely explosive. Industrial machines run under inert gas — argon — specifically so the powder doesn't combust, in sealed enclosures, with trained operators and respirators.

Alex: So when they say "safe for the home workshop" — the safety isn't a feature they bolted on, it's the entire reason these machines cost what they cost?

Jordan: That's exactly it. The enclosure, the gas handling, the powder management — that's most of the machine. You can't subtract it to hit a consumer price and still call it safe. And then there's laser reflection off shiny metal powder, which is its own way to lose an eye. The claims here are flagged dubious for a reason, and I think that's generous.

Alex: And there's no price, no independent verification, just the promise. Which — okay, I'm folding. I came in wanting to believe and you walked me right back out.

Jordan: And look, I'd love to be wrong. If someone genuinely solved safe desktop metal, that's a landmark. But "we made the explosive-powder high-power-laser machine safe and cheap and easy" is the kind of claim that needs third-party testing before it gets a dollar. Remember the Mastrex MX300 we talked about back in episode one — a hundred and eighty-five grand, and that's the cheap end of real metal.

Alex: So the gap between that and a consumer box should tell you everything. This one stays firmly in the "show me, with a safety inspector standing next to it" pile.

Jordan: Show me, with a fire extinguisher and an ophthalmologist on standby.

Alex: Okay, one thing to try this week, straight out of that Hackaday carbon-fiber story. If you print in CF-filled filament and you've been frustrated that your parts snap along the layer lines — try annealing a sacrificial test part. Not your real part. A throwaway.

Jordan: And measure it first. Calipers, write the dimensions down, then anneal it and measure again so you learn exactly how much your specific filament shrinks and warps before you ever risk a part that has to fit something. Low oven temp, go slow, support it flat so it doesn't slump. Treat it like an experiment, because that's what it is.

Alex: Learn the shrinkage on the throwaway so the real one comes out stronger and still fits. That's the whole game. Jordan, this was a good one — fold your composites flat, anneal your test parts, and please do not buy a metal laser printer for your kitchen.
