Layer Lines Weekly — 3D Systems: The Old Guard Bets Everything on Mission-Critical Printing
Episode 14 · 2026-07-28

Alex: This week's company is 3D Systems, and my one-sentence thesis is this: it's the elder statesman of additive manufacturing trying to prove that being old doesn't mean being legacy, by betting its future on printing things that absolutely cannot fail.

Jordan: Mission-critical is the word that keeps showing up. Hypersonic vehicle parts. Dentures. Hearing aids. Parts for an F1 car. There's actually an analyst piece from simplywall.st in January asking whether the company is quietly recasting its entire investment story around mission-critical 3D printing for defense and medical. That's the question we're going to chew on.

Alex: And it's a company with a lot of texture. A forty-year history the company itself touts, a product line that runs from resin printers to triple-laser metal machines, a class action lawsuit, a nineteen percent revenue drop in one quarter, and then a rebound led by healthcare. All of that in the last two years.

Jordan: Also some genuine gaps. The public picture of who's actually steering this company is thinner than you'd expect for a NYSE-listed firm, and we're going to be honest about where our sourcing runs out.

Jordan: One thing we can say with confidence, because it shows up in the moat material: the company's patent portfolio is concentrated in stereolithography. Alex: And SLA is still all over the modern lineup. The brand-new SLA 825 Dual, the Cadillac F1 deal — that's SLA technology. So whatever the precise founding date, the through-line from the earliest days to right now is resin and light.

Jordan: One caveat the fact-checkers raised that I'll pass along: forty-year claims from companies this age can be rounded, or can fold in predecessor entities. Without a precise founding year in our sources, treat it as directionally true rather than an anniversary you'd put on a cake.

Alex: Fair. The honest version of this segment is: very old company, stereolithography roots you can read directly out of its patent portfolio, and an early history we'd love a proper independent source for. If you're a listener with a good archive on this, our inbox is open.

Alex: So let's do what the origin story can't and read the company through its products. I'll start with the one I think best explains modern 3D Systems: Figure 4. There's a customer story on their own site — so, company-controlled, but detailed — about WS Audiology using Figure 4 to make hearing aid components in production-grade plastics, rigid and rubber-like materials.

Jordan: Hearing aids matter as an example because they're the quiet proof case for additive generally. Every ear is different, volumes are high, and the part touches a human body, which means biocompatibility and regulatory scrutiny. If your resin platform survives that environment, you're not a prototyping company anymore. You're a manufacturing company.

Jordan: The other pillar is metal. The DMP Flex 350 Triple — that's their laser powder bed fusion machine with three lasers, which in practice means throughput, more of the build plate being worked at once. The press release says defense manufacturer Elmet Technologies adopted it to produce parts for hypersonic vehicles.

Alex: Hypersonics is about as far from a benchtop resin printer as you can get. What does a part like that demand, practically?

Jordan: Refractory metals, extreme thermal loads, and a qualification burden that makes medical look relaxed. Every parameter locked, every build documented, powder traceability end to end. When a defense supplier picks your machine for that work, it's a statement about process repeatability, not just specs on a datasheet. Though we should note it's a press release, so it's the company's framing of the win.

Alex: Then the new stuff. Trade coverage reports they've launched the SLA 825 Dual and a fleet management software called AddiTrak. The launch is real — our fact-check calls it credible — but there's no independent review yet on whether they actually deliver the workflow improvements claimed. New products, no track record. That's not a knock, just where they are in their life cycle.

Jordan: AddiTrak is the one I'd watch, actually. Fleet management sounds boring until you've run a room with a dozen machines and no unified view of what's printing, what's down, and what's consuming resin. Software like that is how hardware companies try to become sticky.

Alex: Dental deserves its own beat. NextDent is their dental materials brand, and the new thing is the NextDent Jetted Denture solution. Business Wire confirms it was announced with a summer 2026 European launch target — so as we record, it's on the doorstep, not in dental labs at scale.

Jordan: Announced with regulatory clearance behind it, which we'll get into, but yes — its commercial impact is still entirely in front of it. Don't confuse a certification press release with market traction.

Alex: And the fun one: trade coverage from May says the Cadillac Formula 1 team selected 3D Systems' SLA technology for the 2026 season. Wind tunnel models, presumably fast-turnaround aero parts — F1 has always been an SLA showcase.

Jordan: It's a great logo. Whether it moves revenue is a different question — F1 deals are usually more marketing than money. Now, the framing our brief flags as dubious is the 'one-stop shop' positioning: prototyping, tooling, end-use production, all under one roof. That's the company's self-assessment, and there's no independent benchmarking behind it.

Jordan: My practical skepticism: a broad portfolio is only a one-stop shop if the pieces actually integrate. Resin, metal powder bed, dental jetting, and fleet software are very different animals. Is it a platform, or a collection of technologies that share a logo? Our sources don't settle that, and I'd want to see the workflow before I believed the brochure.

Alex: The generous read is that the breadth itself is the story — very few companies can credibly show up in a hearing aid factory, a hypersonics program, and a dental lab in the same year. Whether that breadth is a strength or a focus problem is kind of the question of the whole episode.

What we know: 3D Systems trades on the NYSE under DDD, and per a simplywall.st piece from January, it has significant institutional ownership.

Jordan: We also know, from PR Newswire and Bloomberg Law reporting, that a shareholder class action filed in 2025 names certain officers for allegedly misleading investors about demand. The excerpts we have don't name the individuals, so we won't guess.

Alex: And in July 2026 — this month — the company completed an upsized public offering, per a White & Case note. Upsized means demand let them sell more shares than initially planned. So investors showed up. But an offering also means the company wanted cash.

Jordan: Both readings are legitimate. Upsizing signals appetite; needing to raise at all, after a stretch of declining revenue, can signal that the balance sheet needed help. And existing shareholders get diluted either way. Without an executive on record explaining the use of proceeds in our sourcing, we can't tell you which story is true.

Alex: What strikes me is how strategy-shaped the company's recent moves are, even though we can't see the strategists. Somebody decided to lean into defense facilities, chase EU medical device certification, and stand up a Saudi joint venture. Those are deliberate, expensive, multi-year choices.

Jordan: You can infer a strategy from the fingerprints. What you can't infer is the culture, the succession picture, or how the litigation is landing internally. Anyone who tells you those things without sources is writing fiction.

Jordan: One more inference I'll allow myself: high institutional ownership plus an active class action means this management team operates under real scrutiny. Public-market discipline cuts both ways — it forces accountability, and it can also force short-term thinking exactly when a turnaround needs patience.

Alex: So file leadership under 'visible outputs, invisible people' for this episode. On to what the company is actually good at.

Jordan: My take on the moat: it's less about any single machine and more about three layered things — materials breadth, regulatory clearances, and IP. Machines get copied. Those three are slower to replicate.

Alex: Start with materials. There's a company press release from 2020 announcing a major expansion of the plastics portfolio, and the broader claim in our brief — that the portfolio spans plastics, metals, and biocompatible options — is rated plausible and consistent with what they sell, though not every leg of it is independently evidenced in our packet.

Jordan: Materials are where the recurring revenue lives, and where the switching costs live. If you've qualified a specific resin for a hearing aid shell or a denture base — meaning you've run the testing, filed the paperwork, locked the process — you do not casually swap to a cheaper third-party material. Requalification costs more than the material ever will.

Jordan: Which is why the EU MDR certification matters more than it sounds. Business Wire confirms 3D Systems achieved full-scope certification under the EU's Medical Device Regulation, clearing the path for NextDent dentures in Europe. MDR is a notoriously heavy regime. Companies have exited the European market rather than comply with it.

Alex: Our brief flags the 'high barrier to entry' framing as an interpretation rather than a quantified fact, though. The certification is real; how much protection it buys is the softer claim.

Jordan: A fair flag, and here's my honest read as someone who's watched qualification eat shop-floor budgets: the barrier is real but it's a moat around the market, not around 3D Systems specifically. Competitors can climb the same wall. It buys a head start measured in years, not permanence.

Alex: Then the patents. The stereolithography portfolio is presented as a competitive advantage — company narrative, per our brief — but it's also drawn fire. Trade coverage at 3D Printing Industry from early 2025 details a patent lawsuit accusing 3D Systems of a, quote, brazen anticompetitive scheme.

Jordan: That cuts in an interesting direction. Aggressive enforcement tells you the company believes its patents have teeth. But litigation is also how patents get tested, and the fact-checkers note the risk plainly: an adverse ruling could weaken exactly the IP the moat rests on. A moat you have to defend in court is a moat with a question mark on it.

Alex: And the newest layer is software. AddiTrak — confirmed by their own product page — extends the ecosystem into fleet and workflow management. The open question from our brief is differentiation: how it stacks up against third-party fleet software is unknown.

Jordan: There's also a VoxelMatters comparative piece from May that includes 3D Systems in a discussion of where stereolithography innovation is headed. So independent observers still count them as relevant in their core technology. Not dominant, not dismissed — relevant. That's a fair one-word summary of the moat, honestly.

Alex: Now the map: where does this company actually sit? Reading our brief, three anchors jump out — defense, healthcare and dental, and a genuinely unusual international bet in Saudi Arabia.

Jordan: Defense first. The Elmet hypersonics partnership we covered, and separately, TCT Magazine reported in January that 3D Systems is expanding its Littleton, Colorado facility specifically for defense programs. That's independent trade coverage, and facilities are a costly signal — you don't pour concrete for a market you're dabbling in.

Alex: Healthcare is the one carrying the financials right now. A Quiver Quantitative summary of Q1 2026 earnings says revenue growth was led by healthcare. Jordan: That dental lab example is the kind of evidence I actually trust, by the way. A named lab, a specific outcome — half the production time — reported by a trade outlet rather than the company. That's a customer noticing something real on the floor.

Jordan: The caveat our brief raises on healthcare: we don't have a segment-level revenue breakdown, so 'key growth driver' is directional. And there's a fair question about whether growth is sustainable demand or a bump from regulatory clearances landing all at once.

Alex: Then the wildcard: NAMI, the joint venture in Saudi Arabia. Metal Additive Manufacturing magazine and VoxelMatters both report on it, including the Saudi Electricity Company acquiring a thirty percent stake. That's a state-linked utility buying into a 3D Systems joint venture.

Jordan: It's a distinctive move. Most Western AM companies sell into the Gulf; embedding via a joint venture with strategic local investors is a deeper commitment. The upside is a position in a market that's spending heavily on industrial localization. The risk, which the fact-checkers flag, is that it's early-stage, returns are unproven, and the whole thing rides on Saudi Vision 2030 execution and geopolitics — things entirely outside the company's control.

Alex: Zoom out and the pattern is clear: regulated, qualified, high-stakes verticals. Defense, medical, dental, motorsport. Almost nothing in this brief is about the prototyping and hobbyist end of the market.

Jordan: Which makes sense as a survival strategy. The low end of additive has been commoditized brutally. Regulated verticals are where qualification and materials clearances protect margins. Alex: And the analyst framing supports it — that simplywall.st piece explicitly asks whether 3D Systems is recasting its investment story around mission-critical printing.
